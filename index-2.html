Introduction

Conventional two-dimensional (2D) cell culture systems have been widely used in cancer research due to their simplicity and reproducibility [ref]. However, 2D culture systems have limitations in recapitulating the complex three-dimensional (3D) architecture of solid tumors, including cell–cell interactions, cell–extracellular matrix (ECM) interactions, and the spatial organization of the tumor microenvironment (TME) [ref]. These differences may contribute to discrepancies in drug responses between 2D culture models and in vivo tumors. Therefore, the importance of 3D cell culture models has been increasingly recognized as an alternative approach for investigating tumor biology and evaluating drug responses [ref]. Various 3D culture models, including multicellular spheroids, Matrigel-based systems, and organoids, have been developed. However, Matrigel has several limitations, including its undefined composition, batch-to-batch variability, and limited tunability of mechanical properties [ref].

Gelatin methacryloyl (GelMA) is a photocrosslinkable gelatin derivative that has attracted considerable attention as a promising biomaterial for 3D cell culture and bioprinting [ref]. GelMA retains gelatin-derived cell-adhesive motifs and matrix metalloproteinase (MMP)-sensitive sequences, supporting cell adhesion, proliferation, and ECM remodeling [ref]. In addition, its photocrosslinking properties enable the fabrication of hydrogel structures with tunable mechanical properties [ref]. The GelMA concentration also influences important physicochemical properties of the hydrogel, including stiffness, swelling behavior, pore structure, and mass transport characteristics [ref].

Despite these advantages, the biological properties of GelMA-based 3D constructs are strongly influenced by hydrogel composition and cell culture conditions. In general, increasing GelMA concentration enhances hydrogel stiffness; however, the resulting denser polymer network may restrict pore size and molecular diffusion [ref]. Furthermore, cell distribution and viability within 3D constructs can be affected by local oxygen and nutrient availability. Therefore, establishing suitable hydrogel properties for cell encapsulation and maintaining an appropriate 3D microenvironment are essential for developing reliable 3D cell culture models.

GelMA-based hydrogels offer the potential to recapitulate aspects of the 3D cellular environment and serve as platforms for investigating the tumor microenvironment. In particular, solid tumors exhibit complex spatial structures in which cellular characteristics can be influenced by interactions between tumor cells and the surrounding ECM, as well as by local microenvironmental conditions. Thus, developing 3D culture models that reflect these characteristics is important for studying tumor biology [ref].

Lung cancer is one of the leading causes of cancer-related mortality worldwide [ref], and lung adenocarcinoma is one of its major histological subtypes [ref]. A549 cells, derived from human lung adenocarcinoma, are widely used as an in vitro model for investigating lung cancer biology and drug responses [ref]. In this study, A549 cells were used to establish a GelMA-based 3D tumor culture model. Based on a previously reported cellular density of approximately 1 × 10⁸ cells/mL in lung tumor tissue [ref], a cell seeding density of 3 × 10⁸ cells/mL was selected to facilitate the assessment of cellular distribution and spatial localization within the hydrogel. Furthermore, the physicochemical properties of hydrogels prepared with different GelMA concentrations were evaluated to identify suitable conditions for 3D cell encapsulation. Cellular distribution, morphology, and viability within the hydrogel were also assessed to investigate the potential of GelMA-based hydrogels as reproducible 3D tumor culture platforms for cancer research and drug response evaluation.

Objectives

  • Synthesize GelMA and compare two reaction buffers (PBS and carbonate–bicarbonate, CB).
  • Culture A549 cells in photocrosslinked GelMA and form tumoroids.
  • Test the response of the 3D model to doxorubicin.
  • Prepare the model for bioprinting (in progress).

Methods

GelMA synthesis

Lysine amine groups (–NH₂) of gelatin react with methacrylic anhydride, which attaches methacryloyl groups and releases methacrylic acid. The reaction was run in either PBS or 0.25 M carbonate–bicarbonate buffer (pH above 9). A higher buffer pH keeps more amine groups deprotonated and reactive, so the buffer choice can change the degree of substitution (DS). For reference, a DS of 95.75 ± 0.98% is reported for CB-buffer synthesis in [5].

  • DissolveGelatin dissolved in buffer (PBS or CB).
  • AddMethacrylic anhydride added to the gelatin solution.
  • React50 °C for 90 min.
  • PurifyCentricon filtration, about 4 h. GelMA was used as a solution without freeze-drying.

Synthesis was the most time-consuming part of the workflow.

Gel preparation and cell culture

  • 10% GelMA with LAP photoinitiator at 0.25% or 0.5%.
  • Photocrosslinking at 405 nm. Gels set within 30 s and were fully crosslinked after about 1 min.
  • A549 cells encapsulated at 3×10⁸ cells/mL (tissue cell density is roughly 1×10⁸ cells/mL [14]).
  • Tumoroids were cultured for 2 days before drug treatment.

Drug treatment and analysis

  • Doxorubicin at 0.65, 1.3 and 2.6 µM for 48 h; untreated gels as control.
  • n = 3 gels per group. Relative cell count is normalized to the control.
  • One-way ANOVA with Dunnett's test against the control (***p < 0.001, ****p < 0.0001).

Discussion

  • CB gels held their shape better than PBS gels. This fits the idea that the more basic CB buffer favors a higher degree of methacrylation [5], though DS was not measured here.
  • LAP at 0.25% and 0.5% gave similar gelation behavior.
  • Cell count fell with doxorubicin concentration in CB gels. PBS values are excluded from the drug analysis.
  • Limits: small n (3 per group), a single cell line, no direct measurement of mechanical properties or swelling, and no viability stain yet.

Conclusion

  • CB-based 10% GelMA gelled within 30 s and supported 3D A549 culture.
  • CB gels kept their shape better than PBS gels.
  • Cell count fell dose-dependently with doxorubicin (48 h).
  • Next: bioprinting of GelMA tumoroids.
