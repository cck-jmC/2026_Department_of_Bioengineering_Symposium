Rapid Gelation of Freshly Prepared GelMA for in vitro Assessment of Doxorubicin Response in a 3D A549 Lung Tumor Model Toward Bioprinting Applications

Jimin Cha, Minseo Seo, Hyein Kim, Yongbok Choi & Youngsun Sohn

Department of Biotechnology, The University of Suwon, Hwaseong, Republic of Korea

IntroductionMethodsResultsConclusionDiscussionReferencesTeam

Extended version of the symposium poster. It adds background, method details and figure explanations that did not fit on the poster. Data are preliminary and unpublished.

Introduction

Conventional two-dimensional (2D) cell culture systems have been widely used in cancer research due to their simplicity and reproducibility [1]. However, 2D culture systems have limitations in recapitulating the complex three-dimensional (3D) architecture of solid tumors, including cell–cell interactions, cell–extracellular matrix (ECM) interactions, and the spatial organization of the tumor microenvironment (TME) [1]. These differences may contribute to discrepancies in drug responses between 2D culture models and in vivo tumors. Therefore, the importance of 3D cell culture models has been increasingly recognized as an alternative approach for investigating tumor biology and evaluating drug responses [1,2]. Various 3D culture models, including multicellular spheroids, Matrigel-based systems, and organoids, have been developed. However, Matrigel has several limitations, including its undefined composition, batch-to-batch variability, and limited tunability of mechanical properties [3].

Gelatin methacryloyl (GelMA) is a photocrosslinkable gelatin derivative that has attracted considerable attention as a promising biomaterial for 3D cell culture and bioprinting [4]. GelMA retains gelatin-derived cell-adhesive motifs and matrix metalloproteinase (MMP)-sensitive sequences, supporting cell adhesion, proliferation, and ECM remodeling [4]. In addition, its photocrosslinking properties enable the fabrication of hydrogel structures with tunable mechanical properties [4]. The GelMA concentration also influences important physicochemical properties of the hydrogel, including stiffness, swelling behavior, pore structure, and mass transport characteristics [5].

Despite these advantages, the biological properties of GelMA-based 3D constructs are strongly influenced by hydrogel composition and cell culture conditions. In general, increasing GelMA concentration enhances hydrogel stiffness; however, the resulting denser polymer network may restrict pore size and molecular diffusion [5]. Furthermore, cell distribution and viability within 3D constructs can be affected by local oxygen and nutrient availability. Therefore, establishing suitable hydrogel properties for cell-laden gels and maintaining an appropriate 3D microenvironment are essential for developing reliable 3D cell culture models.

GelMA-based hydrogels offer the potential to recapitulate aspects of the 3D cellular environment and serve as platforms for investigating the tumor microenvironment. In particular, solid tumors exhibit complex spatial structures in which cellular characteristics can be influenced by interactions between tumor cells and the surrounding ECM, as well as by local microenvironmental conditions. Thus, developing 3D culture models that reflect these characteristics is important for studying tumor biology [2,6].

Lung cancer is one of the leading causes of cancer-related mortality worldwide [7], and lung adenocarcinoma is one of its major histological subtypes [8]. A549 cells, derived from human lung adenocarcinoma, are widely used as an in vitro model for investigating lung cancer biology and drug responses [9]. In this study, A549 cells were used to establish a GelMA-based 3D tumor culture model. Based on a previously reported cellular density of approximately 1 × 10⁸ cells/mL in lung tumor tissue [10], the seeding density was optimized to 3 × 10⁸ cells/mL, three times the tissue density, to facilitate the assessment of cellular distribution and spatial localization within the hydrogel. Furthermore, the physicochemical properties of hydrogels prepared with different GelMA concentrations were evaluated to identify suitable conditions for preparing 3D cell-laden gels. Cellular distribution, morphology, and viability within the hydrogel were also assessed to investigate the potential of GelMA-based hydrogels as reproducible 3D tumor culture platforms for cancer research and drug response evaluation.

Objectives

  • Goal: recreate the tumor microenvironment in vitro with a physiologically relevant 3D lung tumor model (tumoroid) that captures the 3D architecture and cell–ECM interactions missing in 2D culture.
  • Platform: establish a reproducible, tunable GelMA-based platform for anticancer drug screening that is compatible with bioprinting.
  • Expected benefit: more predictive drug-response testing before animal studies, potentially reducing cost, time and animal use.
  • This study: compare two reaction buffers, phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) and carbonate–bicarbonate (CB), and two photoinitiator concentrations (LAP, 0.25% and 0.5%); build a 10% GelMA A549 tumoroid at 3 × 10⁸ cells/mL (about three times the tissue density, for clearer observation); and test its response to doxorubicin (0.65–2.6 µM, 48 h).

Materials and Methods

2.1 Scheme of the study workflow

2.2 GelMA synthesis and preparation

Gelatin methacryloyl (GelMA) was synthesized by reacting gelatin with methacrylic anhydride (MAA), which introduces methacryloyl groups onto the lysine amine groups of gelatin and releases methacrylic acid. Gelatin was dissolved in either PBS (pH 7.4) or 0.25 M carbonate–bicarbonate (CB) buffer (pH above 9), MAA was added at 0.1 mL per 1 g of gelatin, and the reaction was carried out at 50 °C for 90 min. The GelMA solution was then purified and concentrated by Centricon filtration for about 4 h and used directly as a ready-to-use gel solution without freeze-drying. The degree of substitution (DS) was not measured in this study; a DS of 95.75 ± 0.98% has been reported for synthesis in 0.25 M CB buffer [11].

Synthesis was the most time-consuming part of the workflow.

2.3 Fabrication of 3D A549 tumoroids

Human lung adenocarcinoma A549 cells (passage 36) were cultured in Dulbecco’s modified Eagle medium (DMEM) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) and 1% penicillin/streptomycin and mixed with GelMA to establish a three-dimensional (3D) tumor model. GelMA solution (10% w/v) containing lithium phenyl-2,4,6-trimethylbenzoylphosphinate (LAP) at 0.25% or 0.5% (w/v) as a photoinitiator was mixed with the cells at 3 × 10⁸ cells/mL, a density optimized at three times that of native tissue (roughly 1 × 10⁸ cells/mL [10]) to allow clearer observation of the cells, and photocrosslinked with 405 nm light in 1.5 mL microcentrifuge (Eppendorf) tubes. Gels set within 30 s and were fully crosslinked after about 1 min. Shape retention of CB-based and PBS-based gels was compared, and the cell-laden gels (A549 tumoroids) were cultured for 2 days before drug treatment.

2.4 Doxorubicin treatment

The A549 tumoroids were treated with doxorubicin at 0.65, 1.3 and 2.6 µM for 48 h to evaluate their response to an anticancer drug. Untreated tumoroids served as controls. Only tumoroids in CB-based gels were analyzed, because cell counts in PBS-based gels were not reliable.

2.5 Microscopic observation and image-based analysis

Tumoroid morphology and cell distribution were evaluated by bright-field microscopy. Cells in the images were counted to quantify changes after doxorubicin treatment, and relative cell counts were normalized to the control.

2.6 Statistical analysis

Data are shown as mean ± standard deviation (SD) with individual data points (n = 3 gels per group). Each treatment group was compared with the control by one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by Dunnett’s multiple comparison test, performed in R 4.6.2 (RStudio). Significance levels are indicated as ***p < 0.001 and ****p < 0.0001.

Results

3.1 Gelation and shape retention of GelMA hydrogels

To find suitable conditions for preparing 3D cell-laden gels, GelMA synthesized in PBS or in 0.25 M CB buffer was crosslinked at 10% (w/v) with LAP at 0.25% or 0.5% under 405 nm light (Figure 3A). In all four conditions the gels began to set within 30 s and were fully crosslinked after about 1 min, so the two LAP concentrations gave similar, rapid gelation at this GelMA concentration. Such fast gelation is useful for preparing cell-laden gels, because cells spend little time in the liquid prepolymer and are held in place before they can settle.

When A549 cells were mixed into the gels, they were evenly distributed (Figure 3B), indicating that the cells were not clumped or sedimented at the bottom during crosslinking. The two buffers differed after 2 days of culture: the CB-based gel kept its round shape better than the PBS-based gel. This difference was also clear after the gels were removed from the tube. The PBS gel (P1) appeared spread out, whereas the CB gel (C1, LAP 0.5%) maintained its shape (Figure 3C). Shape retention matters for a screening platform, because a gel that flattens or deforms changes the cell environment and makes images harder to compare between wells.

3.2 Culture of A549 tumoroids in GelMA

A549 cells were mixed into GelMA at 3 × 10⁸ cells/mL, about three times the reported tissue density of roughly 1 × 10⁸ cells/mL, so that individual cells and their distribution could be seen more clearly. The cell-laden gels were cultured for 2 days in 6-well plates to allow tumoroid formation before any drug was added (Figure 4A). Brightfield microscopy before drug treatment showed A549 cells distributed within the CB-based gel after 2 days of culture (Figure 4C). The same plate after doxorubicin treatment is shown in Figure 4B. This 2-day culture defines the starting condition for the drug experiment described below.

3.3 Cell count decreases with doxorubicin concentration

Treatment with doxorubicin at 0.65, 1.3 and 2.6 µM for 48 h reduced the relative cell count in CB-based gels in a dose-dependent manner (Figure 5). Cell counts were 434, 441 and 430 in the control, 386, 398 and 389 at 0.65 µM, 356, 360 and 350 at 1.3 µM, and 174, 152 and 168 at 2.6 µM (mean ± SD: 435.0 ± 5.6, 391.0 ± 6.2, 355.3 ± 5.0 and 164.7 ± 11.4). Relative to the control, the cell count decreased to 89.9% at 0.65 µM (p = 0.0004), 81.7% at 1.3 µM (p < 0.0001) and 37.9% at 2.6 µM (p < 0.0001) by one-way ANOVA with Dunnett’s test.

The decrease was graded and not linear (Figure 5E, F). Going from 0.65 to 1.3 µM lowered the relative count by only about 8 percentage points, whereas doubling the dose again to 2.6 µM lowered it by about 44 percentage points, leaving fewer than 40% of the control cells. Replicates were tightly grouped (SD of about 5–11 cells per gel), and every dose differed significantly from the control. Because only three concentrations were tested, a half-maximal inhibitory concentration (IC50) cannot be estimated from these data. PBS-based gels were not included in this analysis because their cell counts were not reliable.

3.4 Bioprinting trial with cell-free GelMA

A bioprinting trial was carried out with cell-free GelMA to test whether the ready-to-use gel could serve as a bioink. A 6 mm hollow, dome-down hemisphere with a star pattern on top was designed for a 22G nozzle with 0.3 mm layers (Figure 6B), and cell-free GelMA was printed according to this design (Figure 6A).

The ready-to-use GelMA solution was too low in viscosity to be extruded as it was, so it was first pre-crosslinked once with light. However, light cures only the region that is irradiated, and it cannot reach every reactive group inside the syringe uniformly. The viscosity of the ink was therefore not uniform, and it did not stay constant with time or temperature, which made printing at a constant pressure difficult. Cells were not included in these prints, and cell-laden tumoroids have not yet been printed.

Conclusion

In this study, A549 lung cancer cells were suspended in GelMA and photocrosslinked to establish a 3D lung tumor model, and the model was tested with the anticancer drug doxorubicin. The main findings were as follows.

  1. Buffer choice. GelMA synthesized in 0.25 M carbonate–bicarbonate (CB) buffer, which keeps the reaction at a pH above gelatin’s isoelectric point, is expected to give a high degree of substitution (DS), reported as 95.75 ± 0.98% for this buffer [11]. DS was not measured here.
  2. Gelation and shape. CB-based 10% (w/v) GelMA gelled within 30 s under 405 nm light with either 0.25% or 0.5% LAP, was fully crosslinked after about 1 min, and kept its round shape better than the PBS-based gel after 2 days of culture.
  3. Drug response. A549 tumoroids in CB-based GelMA responded to doxorubicin in a dose-dependent manner. The relative cell count fell to 89.9%, 81.7% and 37.9% of the control at 0.65, 1.3 and 2.6 µM (all p ≤ 0.0004).
  4. Bioprinting trial. A hollow hemisphere was printed with cell-free GelMA after pre-crosslinking, but the ink viscosity was not uniform and printing at a constant pressure was difficult.

Together, these results show that a 3D tumor model can be built from a simple, tunable GelMA gel and can report the effect of an anticancer drug as a change in cell number. The data are preliminary (one cell line, one GelMA concentration, n = 3), and bioprinting of cell-laden tumoroids first requires a more stable and uniform ink viscosity.

Discussion and future work

Why the CB buffer helped

CB-based gels retained their shape better than PBS-based gels. A likely explanation is the degree of substitution (DS). Methacrylation consumes the amine groups of lysine, and as the reaction proceeds methacrylic acid is released, which acidifies the solution. PBS (pH 7.4) cannot hold the pH above gelatin’s isoelectric point (pI, about pH 8–9), so more amine groups become protonated and less reactive. 0.25 M CB buffer (pH above 9) keeps the amines available, and a DS of 95.75 ± 0.98% has been reported for this buffer [11]. A higher DS means more methacryloyl groups per gelatin chain, which can produce a more densely crosslinked and mechanically more stable network. We did not measure DS, stiffness or swelling for our GelMA, so this explanation remains a hypothesis to be confirmed. LAP at 0.25% and 0.5% gave similar gelation at 10% GelMA, so the lower concentration appears sufficient under these conditions.

A simple ready-to-use gel format

Most studies use GelMA as a freeze-dried powder that is re-dissolved before use. Here we instead used a convenient ready-to-use gel (Centricon-purified GelMA solution) directly after synthesis, which removes the freeze-drying and re-dissolving steps and simplifies the preparation of a drug-screening platform. We did not test how long the solution can be stored or how reproducible it is between batches, which would be important for routine use.

Drug response of the tumoroids

Cell numbers decreased with doxorubicin concentration in CB-based gels, so the model can respond to a cytotoxic drug in a dose-dependent manner. The steep drop between 1.3 and 2.6 µM suggests that the effective range of doxorubicin in this gel lies near the upper end of the concentrations tested; testing more concentrations would allow an IC50 to be estimated. Drug penetration through a dense, cell-laden gel can differ from that in 2D culture, and the 3 × 10⁸ cells/mL density used here is higher than the tissue estimate, so the absolute drug sensitivity should not be compared directly with other models. We also did not run a 2D comparison, so we cannot yet say how much more informative the 3D format is for these cells. PBS-based gels were excluded from the drug analysis because their cell counts were not reliable.

Significance

The value of this work lies in showing that a 3D tumor model can be built from a simple, tunable material. Compared with conventional 2D culture, 3D models better reflect cell–matrix interactions and are widely considered more informative for drug testing [1, 2]. Compared with Matrigel, which has an undefined composition and limited tunability [3], GelMA allows stiffness and pore structure to be adjusted through synthesis and concentration conditions [4, 5]. The dose-dependent response shows that the model can report drug effects as changes in cell number, so it could serve as a platform for preliminary screening of anticancer agents.

Take-home: a ready-to-use CB-based GelMA gel is a simple, practical platform for drug-response testing in 3D lung tumor models.

Bioprinting and ink viscosity

In the printing trial, the ready-to-use GelMA solution was too thin to be extruded, so a first light crosslinking step was needed before printing. Because light cures only the irradiated part and cannot reach every reactive group in the syringe uniformly, the ink contained regions of different crosslinking, and its viscosity was not uniform and varied with time and temperature. A constant printing pressure therefore could not give a stable flow. We plan to test alginate as a bioink component to obtain a viscosity that is stable over time and temperature, which will have to be established before cell-laden tumoroids can be printed reproducibly.

Limitations

These results should be regarded as preliminary. Only one cell line (A549) and one GelMA concentration were used, with n = 3 gels per group. Cells were counted from images, no viability stain was used, and there was no control cell line. Neither DS, mechanical properties nor swelling were measured. In the printing trial no cells were included, and the ink viscosity was unstable.

Future work

Bioprinting is the next step. The printing trial showed that a stable and uniform ink viscosity has to be established first, for example by testing alginate as a bioink component. Once this is achieved, GelMA can be used as a bioink, so the same material may be printed into tumoroids of uniform size and cell distribution in multi-well formats, making drug testing more reproducible and scalable. Direct characterization of the GelMA (DS, stiffness, swelling), additional GelMA concentrations, viability assays and more cell lines would strengthen the model. Varying the GelMA concentration would also allow the effect of matrix stiffness and diffusion on drug response to be studied [5]. Because the gel is simple to prepare and printable, the model could further be extended to other drugs, drug combinations, antibody-based agents and co-culture with stromal or immune cells to better represent the tumor microenvironment. These applications remain to be tested.
